## Foreword

The *Rules Cyclopedia*—and the volumes of Basic, Expert, and Companion D&D—arm the adventurer with blades and mail and a handful of gear for the road. Yet the world of the late medieval age was heavier than that: travellers, sellswords, and those who delved beneath the earth carried a far wider array of tools, vessels, and provisions. It is the purpose of this volume to set forth that burden—in gold and in weight—and to give the Dungeon Master the means to rule upon it. Herein are game statistics (Cost in gold pieces, Encumbrance in coin-weights) and concise rules for employing this equipment in play.

**Setting.** Every item in this list belongs to **late medieval Europe in the 15th century**. Materials, forms, and uses reflect what was to be had in that age—or could reasonably be obtained. No anachronisms find a place herein (printed books, modern alloys, rubber, plastic). When in doubt, the referee shall verify against sound historical sources or substitute a period-correct alternative.

**How to use this book.** Part I sets down rules for encumbrance, time, and when gear grants bonuses or penalties. Part II is ordered by category; each category presents a table (Item, Description/Notes, Cost in gp, Enc in en) followed by **detailed descriptions** for each item. Every description comprises:

- **Medieval use** — What the item was used for in that world (cooking, camp, signalling, trade, and the like).
- **Materials** — Of what it was made (iron, copper, leather, wood, clay, horn), fit for the 15th century.
- **Rules** — Game rules: time to use, bonuses or penalties when used aright, breakage or durability, capacity, or other effects at the referee's option.

Encumbrance is given in "en" (coin-weights): 1 en is roughly one-tenth of a pound. These values are compatible with the *Rules Cyclopedia* encumbrance system.

I thank my family and my play groups for their patience and their ear. To all who asked, "Can I buy a shovel?" or "What does a fishhook cost?"—this book is the answer.

**Peter Munkholm**

# Part I: Rules for Use

## 1. Encumbrance

In BECMI and the *Rules Cyclopedia*, the weight and bulk of that which is carried is measured in **encumbrance (en)**. One unit of encumbrance equals roughly one-tenth of a pound—the heft of a typical coin. All encumbrance values in this book follow the same system.

**Movement.** The greater the encumbrance borne, the slower the character shall move. See *Rules Cyclopedia* Chapter 6 (Movement) for the Character Movement Rates and Encumbrance Table and the exact limits (e.g. 400 en for unencumbered movement). The referee shall use that table or the equivalent in his edition.

**Containers.** When an item is placed within a container (sack, chest, backpack), the total encumbrance carried is the container's encumbrance *when empty* plus the encumbrance of all that lies within. A filled waterskin's encumbrance is that of the skin when full (e.g. 30 en), not the empty value.

**Swimming.** Characters bearing more than a moderate encumbrance (e.g. over 400 en) risk sinking when swimming; the referee may rule that heavy loads make swimming impossible or require a saving throw.

## 2. Time and Use

Many tasks consume time. The referee may employ the following guidelines (adjust as he deems fit).

**Rounds and turns.** In combat or exploration, 1 round ≈ 10 seconds; 1 turn ≈ 10 minutes. Thus: driving an iron spike into soft wood might take 1 round; setting a small trap, 1 turn; digging a shallow pit for a trap, several turns or more, as soil and tools allow.

**Digging.** With a proper shovel or spade, a single labourer may excavate roughly 1 cubic foot of loose earth per turn (or half that in hard or stony ground). A pick or mattock hastens work in rocky soil but does not move as much loose dirt per stroke. The referee may require multiple turns or a roll for very large excavations.

**Craft and repair.** Simple repairs (replacing a buckle, sewing a tear) may take 1 turn if the right tools and materials are at hand. More complex work (resetting a trap, repairing a cracked pot with wire) may take longer or require a roll.

**Setting traps.** A small trap (e.g. snare, fox trap) may be set in 1 turn by one familiar with it. A pit trap or deadfall takes longer: digging and camouflaging a small pit may take 1d6 turns or more.

## 3. Bonuses and Penalties

**Right tool for the job.** When a character possesses the appropriate tool and employs it for its intended task, the referee may grant a **+1 bonus** to the relevant roll (e.g. opening a stuck door with a crowbar, sharpening a blade with a whetstone), or may rule that the task takes **half the usual time**, or that a **simple task succeeds automatically** without a roll.

**Improvised tools.** Employing the wrong tool (e.g. a dagger in place of a chisel, or a rock in place of a hammer) may impose a **-1 or -2 penalty** to the roll, or **double the time** required, at the referee's discretion.

**No tool.** Attempting a task without any tool when one would ordinarily be expected (e.g. digging with bare hands, cutting rope with teeth) may result in automatic failure, a stiff penalty, or extra time.

## 4. Durability (Optional)

The referee may rule that tools and gear can be damaged or destroyed.

**Critical failure.** If a roll for using a tool is a natural 1 (or otherwise a critical failure), the referee shall roll 1d6: on a result of 1, the tool is broken or ruined and must be replaced. On 2–6, it is merely dulled, bent, or chipped and may yet function with a penalty until repaired.

**Misuse.** Using a tool for that for which it was not designed (e.g. a fine needle as a pry bar) may increase the chance of breakage.

**Repair.** Simple repairs may be possible in town or with spare parts; the cost and time are left to the referee to determine.

## 5. Availability

Not every item is for sale in every place. The referee may restrict what is available by location:

- **Village or hamlet:** Basic food, simple containers, common tools (rope, sacks, simple knives, perhaps a shovel or axe). No luxury goods or specialised equipment.
- **Small town:** Most items in this book that were common in the 15th century. Smiths, chandlers, and merchants can supply cooking gear, nails, traps, cordage, and the like.
- **City or large town:** Full range of equipment, including better quality and rarer items (e.g. fine parchment, good locks, specialised traps).
- **Remote areas:** Only what can be carried in or made locally; prices may be higher.

The referee may also roll for availability: e.g. on 1d6, 1–4 = in stock, 5 = available in 1d6 days, 6 = not available.

**Prices and quality.** Where Cost is given as a range (e.g. 1–5 gp), the lower end is basic or common quality and the higher end is fine or rare; the referee shall set the exact cost by location, quality, or roll (e.g. 1d6: 1–2 = low, 3–4 = mid, 5–6 = high).

**Optional rules in this book.** The following are optional: durability and critical-failure breakage (Section 4); variable breakage on critical failure when using tools; and "the referee may rule" bonuses or penalties in Part II. The referee may ignore or simplify these as he deems fit.

## 6. Worked Examples

**Example 1: Digging a pit trap.** The party wishes to dig a pit 5' deep and 3' × 3' in soft earth. Volume ≈ 45 cubic feet. With one shovel and one labourer, at 1 cubic foot per turn, that is 45 turns (about 7–8 hours of work). The referee may allow two characters with shovels to halve the time. A pick is not much faster for loose soil but avails if they hit stones.

**Example 2: Fixing a door.** A wooden door has come off its hinges. The party has a hammer, 6 iron nails, and a wedge. The referee rules that with the right tools, rehanging the door takes 2 turns and succeeds automatically (no roll). Without a hammer, it may take 4 turns and require a roll; without nails, they must find another way (e.g. rope, or new hinges from town).

**Example 3: Setting a fox trap.** A character with a purchased fox trap and 1 turn to set it places it in a likely spot. The referee rules that no roll is needed to set it; a roll may be required later to determine whether an animal is caught, or the referee may decide by narrative. If the character had no trap and sought to improvise a snare, the referee may require a roll and 2 turns, with a -1 penalty without proper cord or experience.

**Pot, earthenware.**  
*Medieval use:* In peasant crofts and town kitchens alike, the earthen pot hung over the fire—pottage and porridge, beans and barley. Glaze held the broth; without it, the clay drank the liquor and left a taste of earth. Set in the coals or on a trivet; drop it, and the crack could spell a cold supper and a lost vessel.  
*Materials:* Fired clay; glaze (when present) lead-based or ash-based. Hand-thrown or moulded; the walls thick enough to take the heat.  
*Rules:* Suitable for boiling and stewing. If heated empty or over a fierce flame, or if dropped, the referee shall roll 1d6: on a result of 1, the pot cracks and is ruined. Unglazed pots absorb flavours; therein, contents may taint if used for different foods without washing.

**Pot, copper.**  
*Medieval use:* The copper pot belonged to the better kitchen and the apothecary's bench—it heated quick and even, and did not quarrel with many preparations. Sauces, preserves, delicate work. But leave it dirty or wet, and the green bloom of verdigris appeared; men said it was poison.  
*Materials:* Hammered or cast copper; sometimes tin-lined (pewter) for acid.  
*Rules:* +1 to any roll involving even heating or precise temperature (at the referee's option). If left dirty or wet, verdigris may form; the referee may rule contents spoiled or unusable after one week neglected.

**Pot, cast iron.**  
*Medieval use:* From the 14th century onward, the north knew the iron pot—slow boil, stew, and bread buried in coals. It outlasted a man; it was passed down. It wanted seasoning, oil rubbed in and heated, or rust would take it.  
*Materials:* Cast iron; thick walls that held the heat. Wood or rope handle—the rope could char.  
*Rules:* Holds heat well; contents stay warm 1 turn after removal from fire. If not seasoned or left wet, rust may appear; the referee may rule -1 to durability or unusable for food until re-seasoned. May be used as improvised weapon (1d3 damage) at the referee's option.

**Cauldron.**  
*Medieval use:* The great pot of the camp and the manor—meat and broth, small beer, and in the workshop, dye and soap. It hung from trammel or tripod over the fire; it fed many mouths. Empty, a man could carry the small one; the large wanted two backs or a beast.  
*Materials:* Wrought or cast iron; three legs or hanging ears.  
*Rules:* Small cauldron (2 gallon) may be carried when empty; large (5 gallon) typically requires two persons or a pack animal. Full encumbrance = pot + contents (1 gallon water ≈ 80 en). Setting up over fire: 1 turn with tripod or trammel.

**Frying pan.**  
*Medieval use:* Fish in the pan, eggs, fritters; flatbread and small cakes laid direct on the iron over coals. Town and country both knew it by the 15th century. Copper made a lighter pan for those who could pay.  
*Materials:* Wrought or cast iron; long handle of wood or iron. Copper versions lighter; used in wealthy kitchens.  
*Rules:* Iron pan may serve as a shield against fire (the referee may grant +1 to save vs. breath weapon or the like). Copper pan: encumbrance as listed; no special rule.

**Jar, earthenware / glass.**  
*Medieval use:* Earthenware held flour, salt, herbs, honey, oil, and preserves in pantry and workshop. Glass—costly—let the apothecary see his simples and kept them from reacting with the vessel; it was fragile.  
*Materials:* Clay, fired and often glazed; or blown glass with cork or wax stopper.  
*Rules:* Glass jar: if dropped or struck, the referee shall roll 1d6; on 1–2, the jar breaks and contents are lost or damaged. Earthenware: on 1, breaks; contents may be salvaged if not liquid.

**Cask.**  
*Medieval use:* Wood bore the wine, the beer, the water, and the grain on cart and ship. Coopers made standard sizes; the small cask rode with the traveller. Let it dry and the staves shrank—then it leaked until swelled again.  
*Materials:* Oak or other hardwood staves; iron or wooden hoops.  
*Rules:* When full of liquid, encumbrance = cask + contents (e.g. 1 gallon water ≈ 80 en; 5 gallons ≈ 400 en). The cask must be kept moist when empty or the staves may shrink and leak; the referee may rule one week dry requires swelling before use.

## 8. Tent and Camp

The road offered little shelter—canvas and wool kept the rain off and the cold at bay. Soldier and pilgrim alike slept under hemp or linen; the wealthy had leather and pavilions. Peg and mallet and guy line made the difference between a dry night and a long, wet dark.

**Ground cloth.**  
*Medieval use:* Between the sleeper and the earth—waxed or oiled canvas kept the wet out and the cold at bay. Soldier and pilgrim laid it under tent or bedroll; without it, mud seeped through and vermin found the warmth. Nobles had finer cloth; the road had hemp and beeswax.  
*Materials:* Hemp or linen canvas; wax (beeswax) or oil (linseed) for waterproofing—the smell of tallow and earth when unrolled.  
*Rules:* If used under tent, reduces chance of damp or vermin (the referee may grant +1 to save vs. disease or rest quality). Without ground cloth, the referee may rule wet ground causes fatigue or illness after 1d6 nights.

**Tent, 1–2 person.**  
*Medieval use:* The road offered little shelter—canvas and pole and the skill to pitch them. Lean-to or small dome; one or two bodies inside, the wind on the walls. Soldiers and pilgrims and merchants knew it; peg and guy line made the difference between a dry night and a long, wet dark.  
*Materials:* Canvas (hemp or linen), sometimes waxed; wooden pole(s); rope or leather guylines; wooden or iron pegs. Without the pegs, the cloth was so much dead weight.  
*Rules:* Setup: 2 turns with one person; 1 turn with two. Requires pegs and guylines (sold separately or in set). Without pegs and lines, the referee shall roll 1d6 each night in strong wind: on 1–2, the tent collapses. Capacity: 1–2 persons; encumbrance is for packed tent.

**Tent, 4–6 person.**  
*Medieval use:* Larger shelter for the party or the retinue—commanders and wealthy travellers paid the price in gold and in labour. More canvas, more poles, more hands to pitch and strike. In camp it was home; on the march it rode the pack beast or was shared among backs.  
*Materials:* Heavy canvas; multiple poles; iron pegs and stout guylines. The weight of a small man when packed.  
*Rules:* Setup: 4 turns with two persons, or 2 turns with four. Encumbrance is high; typically carried on pack animal or shared among party. Without proper pegging, the referee may rule collapse on 1–3 on 1d6 in wind.

**Tent peg, mallet, guy line.**  
*Medieval use:* Wood held the line taut—until the ground was hard or the wind rose, and then iron held where wood pulled out. The mallet drove the peg without splitting it; without the mallet, a stone could do, but the peg might crack. Every tent in the wind wanted pegs and lines; without them, the cloth was a sail.  
*Materials:* Pegs: hardwood or iron. Mallet: wood. Guy line: hemp rope or leather.  
*Rules:* Without pegs and lines, the tent cannot be pitched securely. With mallet, driving 6 pegs takes 1 turn. Iron pegs: +1 to avoid pulling out in wind (at the referee's option).

## 9. Fishing

The monk at the pond, the peasant at the stream—rod and line and hook fed many. Nets took the bulk catch; the creel carried the day's take. Gut and hemp and lead and cork; the fish did not give themselves freely.

**Fishing rod.**  
*Medieval use:* The monk at the pond, the peasant at the stream—rod and line and hook fed many. Hazel, willow, or ash cut to length; jointed or collapsible for the traveller. The line ran to the tip; float and sinker were the angler's choice. Patience and the right run; the fish did not give themselves freely.  
*Materials:* Wood (hazel, willow, ash); line of gut, hemp, or flax. The rod bent when the fish took.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 turn or more per fishing attempt; the referee may require a roll (e.g. 1d6, 1–2 = catch in good waters) or rule by narrative. With correct bait and conditions, the referee may grant +1. In poor waters or wrong season, -1 or no catch.

**Fishhook and line.**  
*Medieval use:* Hand-forged iron in every size—the hook that held the gut or hemp. Gut was strong and nearly invisible in the water; hemp was cheaper and rotted sooner. From bank or boat the line went down; one snag could cost the hook and the catch.  
*Materials:* Iron hook; gut, hemp, or flax line.  
*Rules:* Single hook: 0 en; set of 12: 2 en. Line (50') is 2 en. On 1 on 1d6 when snagged, the hook is lost (at the referee's option). Silver hook (×10 cost) may be required for certain supernatural creatures; the referee shall determine.

**Net.**  
*Medieval use:* Small hand nets to land the hooked fish or scoop the shallow run; the large net wanted two backs and a haul—seine or cast, the bulk catch. Regulated in many regions; the lord's stream and the common pond. Cord and knot and cork and lead; the fish did not give themselves in bulk without a net.  
*Materials:* Hemp or flax cord; knotted by hand. Floats (cork or wood) and weights (lead or stone) on the larger nets.  
*Rules:* Small net: 1 person, 1 turn to dip or land fish. Large net: 2+ persons, 2 turns to cast and haul; the referee may rule 1d6 × 10 fish in good waters, or 0 in poor. Net can entangle Small creature if used as weapon (treat as net weapon from *Rules Cyclopedia*).

**Creel.**  
*Medieval use:* Wicker on the back or at the belt—the day's take went in, the water drained out, the air moved through. Fish stayed cool and edible until evening; without the creel, they spoiled by noon. The angler's pride and the monk's larder.  
*Materials:* Woven willow or hazel; leather strap.  
*Rules:* Holds up to 20 en of fish; encumbrance is creel (15 en) + contents. Fish in creel stay fresh 1 day in normal temperatures; the referee may extend in cold weather.

**Float.**  
*Medieval use:* Cork or wood on the line—the bait hung at depth, the bite showed as a dip or a run. Still water and slow current; the float was the angler's eye below the surface.  
*Materials:* Cork or light wood; tied to line. Negligible weight.  
*Rules:* Encumbrance 0 en (negligible). The referee may rule float grants +1 to notice a bite (at the referee's option). See Section 33 (Fishing Supplementary) for extended use.

**Sinker.**  
*Medieval use:* Lead or stone on the line—sank the bait, held it in the current. Bottom fishing in stream or river; without weight, the line drifted and the hook rode too high.  
*Materials:* Lead (cast) or stone; tied to line.  
*Rules:* Encumbrance 1 en. The referee may rule sinker required for fishing in current (at the referee's option). See Section 33.

**Bait box.**  
*Medieval use:* Wood or leather, vented—worms and grubs and what the fish would take. Kept bait alive or fresh on the trail; a day without the box and the bait was dead or stinking. Angler and hunter both carried one.  
*Materials:* Wood (with ventilation) or leather; sometimes damp moss or cloth inside.  
*Rules:* The referee may rule bait lasts 1 day without box, 3 days with (at the referee's option). Encumbrance 5 en. See Section 33 for full description.

**Needle and thread.**  
*Medieval use:* A torn cloak, a burst sack—the road wore gear to rags. Needle and thread could mend a little; iron or bronze for the needle, linen or wool for the seam. Silk was imported and costly, for fine work and embroidery. Every traveller who hoped to last carried the means to repair.  
*Materials:* Needle: iron or bronze; hand-forged, easy to lose in the straw. Thread: linen, wool, or silk; wound on a spool or bobbin.  
*Rules:* Single needle: 0 en; set of six: 1 en. Thread spool: 1 en. Use: 1 turn to mend small tear; the referee may require a roll for delicate or large repair. Silk thread: +1 to repair fine cloth or leather (at the referee's option).

**Yarn.**  
*Medieval use:* Wool or linen, heavier than thread—for knitting, darning, and binding. Socks and caps and patches for thick cloth; the ball or skein went into the pack. One ball could mean ten patches or a pair of socks; the traveller who ran out had cold feet and ragged cuffs.  
*Materials:* Wool (most common), linen, or mixed. Dyed or natural.  
*Rules:* One ball: 10 en. Use: 1 turn per small repair; the referee may rule 1 ball = 10 patches or 1 pair of socks.

**Awl.**  
*Medieval use:* The pointed tool that went through leather before the thread—cobblers and saddlers and every traveller with a boot or strap to mend. Pierce, then stitch; without the awl, the needle bent or the leather tore. Essential for leatherwork.  
*Materials:* Iron or bronze point; wooden or bone handle.  
*Rules:* Use with thread or lace to sew leather. +1 to repair leather items (at the referee's option). Without awl, the referee may impose -1 or double time for leather repair.

*Medieval use:* The march lived on what would keep—ship biscuit, twice-baked bread, the standard ration for soldier, sailor, and pilgrim. Kept for months if kept dry; eaten with water, beer, or in pottage. Boring but filling and cheap; wet it and it turned to mush, and mould could kill.  
*Materials:* Flour (wheat, barley, or rye), water, salt; baked twice to drive out moisture.  
*Rules:* Keeps 2 months if dry; 1 week in damp (at the referee's option). One week ration: 21 meals; encumbrance 35 en. If wet or mouldy, the referee may rule unfit to eat (save vs. poison or illness).

**Dried meat and fish.**  
*Medieval use:* Salted or smoked—beef, pork, stockfish (dried cod), salted herring. The main preserved protein for travel and winter; eaten with bread or in pottage. Stockfish was common in the north and wanted soaking before cooking, or it was hard as wood.  
*Materials:* Meat or fish; salt or smoke preserved.  
*Rules:* One week ration: 25 en (meat) or 20 en (fish). Keeps 2 months if dry; 1 week in damp. The referee may rule stockfish requires 1 turn soaking before cooking.

**Cheese.**  
*Medieval use:* Hard cheese—aged cow or sheep—travelled well and fed soldiers and pilgrims. Soft cheese spoiled in days unless carried cool or eaten at once. One wrong bite and the belly turned; the road had no mercy for bad food.  
*Materials:* Milk (cow, sheep, or goat); rennet; salt.  
*Rules:* Hard cheese: keeps 2 weeks in normal travel; 1 week in heat. Soft cheese: 3 days. One week ration: 30 en. The referee may rule spoiled cheese causes save vs. poison.

**Honey.**  
*Medieval use:* Sweetener, preservative for fruit and meat, and in medicines and wound dressings. Stored in pottery or in the comb; beekeeping was widespread. Honey in the wound could keep the rot at bay—or so the healers said.  
*Materials:* Honey; jar or comb.  
*Rules:* 1 lb: 15 en. Keeps indefinitely if sealed. Can be used as antiseptic on wounds (the referee may grant +1 to save vs. infection). As ration: 1 lb ≈ 3 days' sweetening or energy (at the referee's option).

**Wine and ale.**  
*Medieval use:* Wine in skins or bottles, ale in jugs or casks—the standard drink for travel when water was unsafe. Ale was weaker and drunk daily; wine was for those who could pay. Ale in a skin spoiled soon; wine kept for months.  
*Materials:* Wine: grape; ale: barley and water. Containers: leather, clay, or wood.  
*Rules:* Encumbrance includes container when full (e.g. 1 quart wine in skin: 30 en). Wine keeps months; ale spoils in days unless casked. The referee may rule ale in skin spoils in 1 week.

**Salt, spices, butter, bread.**  
*Medieval use:* Salt preserved food and was traded widely—mined or evaporated, worth its weight on the road. Spices were luxury; pepper and the like came from afar. Butter in the crock for cooking and eating; it turned in the heat. Fresh bread did not keep—eaten the same day or it went hard or mouldy.  
*Materials:* Salt: mined or evaporated. Spices: imported. Butter: churned cream. Bread: flour, water, leaven.  
*Rules:* Salt: 1 lb keeps indefinitely; 10 en. Spices: 1–5 gp per pouch; 2 en. Butter: spoils in 1 week in heat (at the referee's option). Fresh bread: 1 day; 5 en per person per day.

**Shovel.**  
*Medieval use:* Broad blade for loose earth, snow, grain, or manure. Wood shovels were cheaper—farm and croft; iron-bladed shovels dug trenches and pits for soldiers and labourers. One cubic foot per turn in soft ground; half that when the earth was hard or stony. Drop the wood blade on a rock and it could split.  
*Materials:* Wood: ash or oak blade and haft. Iron: wrought-iron blade; wooden haft (ash or hazel).  
*Rules:* Digging: 1 cubic foot of loose earth per turn (1 person); half that in hard or stony ground. With correct shovel, the referee may grant +1 to digging rolls or halve time in soft soil. Wood shovel: on critical failure (1 on 1d6), the referee may rule blade splits; iron shovel: no breakage from normal use.

**Spade.**  
*Medieval use:* Narrower than the shovel—for cutting sod, digging trenches, and edging. Neat holes and clean sides; the soldier's trench and the gardener's bed. The stepped blade took the foot; the turf came up in strips.  
*Materials:* Wrought-iron blade; wooden haft. Blade often stepped (foot rest).  
*Rules:* Digging: 1 cubic foot per turn in loose earth; same as shovel in turf (the referee may rule spade +1 in turf). Preferred for pit traps with neat sides (at the referee's option).

**Mattock.**  
*Medieval use:* One end pick, one end adze—break hard or stony soil, chop roots, dig where the shovel stuck. Standard for miners and labourers; ideal for pit traps in rocky or root-bound ground. The heft of iron and the bite of the blade.  
*Materials:* Wrought-iron head; wooden haft (ash or oak).  
*Rules:* In hard or stony ground, mattock digs 1 cubic foot per turn (shovel would take 2 turns). +1 to digging rolls in rocky or root-bound ground (at the referee's option). Can be used as improvised weapon (1d4 damage; at the referee's option).

**Pick.**  
*Medieval use:* Pointed end(s) for rock and hard earth. Light pick (one-handed) for miners and masons; heavy pick (two-handed) for quarrying and siege work. In loose earth the shovel was faster; in stone the pick was the only way.  
*Materials:* Wrought-iron head; wooden haft. Light: short haft; heavy: long haft.  
*Rules:* Light pick: 1 cubic foot of rock per 2 turns (1 person). Heavy pick: 1 cubic foot per 1 turn. In loose earth, shovel or spade is faster. Can be used as improvised weapon (light: 1d4; heavy: 1d6; at the referee's option).

**Auger, earth.**  
*Medieval use:* Wooden or metal screw or spoon for boring holes in soil—posts, stakes, planting. Smaller and lighter than a full shovel; the gardener's and labourer's tool for setting a stake or a trap trigger without moving a mountain of earth.  
*Materials:* Wood: hardwood (oak, ash) screw or spoon. Metal: wrought-iron bit; wooden handle (T-handle or brace).  
*Rules:* Boring: 1 hole (1' deep, 3" diameter) per turn in soft soil; 2 turns in hard soil. Use: setting stakes, posts, or trap triggers. Encumbrance 30 en; lighter than shovel (60 en).

*Medieval use:* Hide glue—animal skin and bone—bonded wood; it had to be heated to melt and applied warm. Tar (pine or birch) preserved rope and leather and sealed seams. Wax (beeswax) for seals, polish, and waterproofing. Cold glue was useless; wet tar stank but held.  
*Materials:* Hide glue: animal collagen; heated in pot. Tar: distilled wood; pot or brush. Wax: beeswax.  
*Rules:* Hide glue: heat 1 turn; apply 1 turn; bond holds 1d6 days unless wet (the referee may extend). Tar: 1 turn to apply; rope or leather +1 to save vs. rot (at the referee's option). Wax: 1 turn to seal document or waterproof; seal holds until broken.

## 19. Traps

The iron jaw in the run, the noose in the dark—game for the pot or a foe in the pit. By the late medieval age the spring-jaw had come to the hunt and the war; the snare and the deadfall were older than memory.

**Fox trap.**  
*Medieval use:* The iron gin—spring jaws that closed on the leg when the beast trod the plate. Set in a run or by bait; the fox, the otter, the cat. Hunters and furriers knew it; so did the poacher. One wrong step and the bite could lame.  
*Materials:* Wrought iron; iron spring; trigger plate.  
*Rules:* Setting: 1 turn. Holds Small creature (e.g. fox, cat). The referee may require a roll (1d6, 1–2 = catch in good placement) or rule by narrative. Escape: roll vs. Strength or use tool (the referee shall determine); 1d6 rounds to free. Trap can be reused; on a result of 1 on 1d6 when setting, the referee may rule the spring fails and the trap does not fire.

**Bear trap.**  
*Medieval use:* The great jaw—bear, wolf, boar. It weighed heavy; it wanted two hands to set, and a careless finger could lose a bone. Professional hunters and those who defended livestock bore it.  
*Materials:* Wrought iron; heavy spring; broad jaws.  
*Rules:* Setting: 2 turns (1 person) or 1 turn (2 persons). Holds Medium creature (e.g. wolf, man). Escape: as fox trap but +2 to difficulty (at the referee's option). Damage: 1d4 per round held (at the referee's option). Trap can be reused.

**Snare.**  
*Medieval use:* A loop of cord or wire in the run; the rabbit or the bird stepped in and the noose drew tight. Placement wanted skill—the right run, the right height. The oldest trap.  
*Materials:* Cord (hemp, flax) or iron wire; noose and anchor.  
*Rules:* Setting: 1 turn per snare. Effectiveness: the referee may roll 1d6 (1–2 = catch in good run) or rule by narrative. Snare holds Small creature; escape as fox trap. Set of 5: 10 en.

**Pit spikes and cover.**  
*Medieval use:* Iron spikes driven into the pit bottom—the fall did the rest. Branches and cloth and dirt hid the hole and bore a man's weight until he stepped true; then the earth gave way. Hunting and defence alike. The pit must be dug first (see Digging).  
*Materials:* Wrought-iron spikes; wood or cloth cover; camouflage (leaves, dirt).  
*Rules:* The pit must be dug first (see Section 13). Spikes: 6 per 40 en; drive into pit bottom (1 turn per 2 spikes). Cover: 3' × 3' supports 1 man-sized creature; when weight is applied, the referee shall roll 1d6 (1–2 = cover holds 1 round, 3–6 = collapse). Fall: 1d6 damage per 5' depth + 1d4 per spike (at the referee's option).

**Caltrops.**  
*Medieval use:* Four points of iron—cast so one always faced the sky. Scattered before the gate or across the path; the horse went lame, the bare foot bled. Late medieval warfare knew them well.  
*Materials:* Wrought iron; four points ~1" long.  
*Rules:* Scatter: 1 round. 4 caltrops cover ~1 square; 20 cover 10' × 10'. Creature moving through: the referee may rule 1d2 or 1d3 damage to the foot and movement halved until rest (1 turn). Horses and bare feet are vulnerable; armoured boots may receive +1 save (at the referee's option). Caltrops can be gathered and reused.

## 20. Light and Fire

Light was life in the deep—tallow smoked, oil burned, and a dropped torch could spell darkness and worse. Candles, lamps, and torches were standard in the 15th century; tinderbox and flint-and-steel for ignition. The traveller who lost his fire lost his warmth and his sight.

**Candle.**  
*Medieval use:* Tallow (animal fat) or beeswax—burned about an hour. Tallow was cheap and smoky; beeswax burned longer and cleaner. Indoors and in lanterns; in wind the flame guttered and could die.  
*Materials:* Tallow or beeswax; wick (linen or cotton).

**Lamp and lantern.**  
*Medieval use:* Oil lamp—clay or metal, wick—burned oil for four hours. Hooded lantern protected the flame from wind; bullseye (lens or slot) focused light in a beam—signalling or narrow passages. Indoors and on the road; the traveller who ran out of oil walked in the dark.

**Tinderbox.**

*Materials:* Wood or metal box; flint; steel striker; tinder (charcloth, amadou, or tow).  
*Rules:* Light fire: 1 round per attempt. The referee may require 1d6 (1–2 = success in normal conditions; 3–4 = success in dry; 5–6 = fail, try again). Wet conditions: -1 or -2 to roll (at the referee's option). Fire steel alone (no tinder): -2 to roll; tinder required. Tinder: 1 oz lasts 10 lights (at the referee's option).

**Bellows, small.**  
*Medieval use:* Hand-held bellows—leather and wood—for blowing air into forge or campfire. Increased heat and speed of lighting; smiths and camp cooks knew the pump and the blast. Without bellows, the fire took longer to catch.  
*Materials:* Leather (two panels); wooden nozzle; metal or wood mouth.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 round to pump. The referee may rule bellows grant +1 to light fire or reduce time to get coals hot (e.g. half time; at the referee's option). Encumbrance 20 en.

*Rules:* +1 to avoid getting lost in open terrain (at the referee's option). Useless in heavy magnetic disturbance (the referee shall determine). Encumbrance 5 en.

**Hourglass.**  
*Medieval use:* Sand from upper bulb to lower—watches at sea, cooking time, sermon length. Common in ships and households by the 15th century. Tilt it and the measure was false.  
*Materials:* Glass bulbs; wood or metal frame; fine sand.  
*Rules:* Measures 1 hour (or other interval if specified). Must be level; the referee may rule tilt causes inaccurate measure. Encumbrance 15 en.

**Sun dial, pocket.**  
*Medieval use:* Foldable dial with gnomon—sun's shadow when unfolded and level. Travellers and scholars when the sun was visible; cloud or night and it was useless.  
*Materials:* Wood, brass, or ivory; engraved with hour lines.  
*Rules:* Works only in daylight with clear sun. Use: 1 round to set level and read. The referee may grant +1 to know time of day for scheduling. Encumbrance 5 en.

**Waterproof bag.**  
*Medieval use:* Waxed canvas or leather—documents, tinder, or food dry in rain or river crossing. Messengers, travellers, and sailors. A torn seal and the contents were wet.  
*Materials:* Canvas (hemp, linen) or leather; wax (beeswax) or oil for waterproofing.  
*Rules:* Capacity ~50 en. Contents inside get +1 to save vs. water damage (at the referee's option). The referee may rule bag must be kept sealed (cord or tie). Encumbrance 8 en empty.

**Strap, spare (3).**  
*Medieval use:* Extra leather—repair, lashing loads, replacing broken harness. Travellers and teamsters carried spares; a broken strap could drop the load or lame the beast.  
*Materials:* Leather (calf or goat); buckles or ties.  
*Rules:* Three straps, 5' total; 5 en. Use: 1 turn to replace broken strap or lash load. The referee may rule one strap = 1 repair for saddle, pack, or armour.

**Buttons (6).**  
*Medieval use:* Bone, wood, or metal—securing clothes. Sold in sets; spare buttons for repair. The missing button in formal company could cost a reaction.  
*Materials:* Bone, wood, horn, or metal (tin, bronze).  
*Rules:* Set of 6: 0 en (negligible). Use: 1 round to sew one button. The referee may rule missing button causes -1 to reaction in formal situations.

**Key, blank.**  
*Medieval use:* Uncut blank for the locksmith to file to match a specific lock. Carried when a duplicate was needed or when the lock lay at the destination.  
*Materials:* Wrought iron or bronze; blank bit and bow.  
*Rules:* Locksmith must cut to match lock; cost and time as the referee rules (e.g. 1 gp, 1 day). Encumbrance 2 en.

**Padlock.**  
*Medieval use:* Portable lock with unique key—chests, doors, chain, or hasp. Common in towns and on transport. Lose the key and the smith or the break-open was the only way.  
*Materials:* Wrought iron; tumbler or warded mechanism; iron key.  
*Rules:* Picking requires thief or smith; the referee may rule break-open damages lock or chest. Key is unique; duplicate requires blank and locksmith. Encumbrance 25 en.

**Chain, 5' (light).**  
*Medieval use:* Iron links—tie doors closed, secure loads, restrain prisoners. Light chain was thinner; a file could cut it in time.  
*Materials:* Wrought iron; hand-forged links.  
*Rules:* 5' length: 50 en. Can restrain one man-sized creature; escape may require pick, key, or break (the referee shall determine). The referee may rule file cuts chain in 1d6 turns.

**Manacles.**  
*Medieval use:* Iron cuffs for wrists or ankles—linked by short chain. Guards, slavers, and those transporting prisoners. Lose the key and the prisoner stayed bound until the smith came.  
*Materials:* Wrought iron; hinge and hasp; key.  
*Rules:* Restrains one person (wrists or ankles). Escape: pick lock, key, or break; the referee shall set difficulty. Encumbrance 30 en. Key is separate; losing it requires smith.

**Marker stake.**  
*Medieval use:* Wooden stake or post—cloth or flag optional—to mark trail, camp, or boundary. Scouts and surveyors; the wrong mark could lead the party astray.  
*Materials:* Wood (hazel, oak); optional cloth or cord.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 round to drive into ground (hammer or mallet). The referee may grant +1 to find way back or to signal location. Encumbrance 5 en.

**Barrel, small.**  
*Medieval use:* Wooden barrel—water, wine, or dry goods; 5-gallon size portable on cart or by two backs. Coopers made standard sizes. Let it dry and the staves shrank and it leaked.  
*Materials:* Oak or other hardwood staves; iron or wood hoops.  
*Rules:* Capacity ~5 gallons (20 quarts). Empty: 60 en; full of water: 60 + 400 = 460 en (1 gallon ≈ 80 en). Must be kept moist when empty or the referee may rule staves shrink and leak.

**Bucket.**  
*Medieval use:* Wood or metal—water, grain, or camp use. Standard 2-gallon for wells and chores.  
*Materials:* Wood (stave or coopered) or metal (tin, iron).  
*Rules:* Capacity ~2 gallons. Empty: 25 en; full of water: 25 + 160 = 185 en. Can be used to carry water, sand (for fire), or small items.

**Ladle.**  
*Medieval use:* Wood or metal—serving stew, soup, or drink from pot or barrel. Camp and kitchen; the shared pot wanted a ladle or the fingers burned.  
*Materials:* Wood (carved) or metal (tin, iron); handle 6–12".  
*Rules:* Capacity ~1 quart. Encumbrance 5 en. No special rules; required for hygienic serving from shared pot (at the referee's option).

*Rules:* Encumbrance 8 en. Use: attach to bridle or halter; one hand occupied when leading. The referee may rule lead rope can be used for tying (e.g. to post).

*Rules:* Use: 1 turn to apply. The referee may rule limb heals normally if set and rested (see *Rules Cyclopedia* or the referee's ruling). Encumbrance 10 en.

**Tourniquet.**  
*Medieval use:* Cord or leather strap tightened above wound to stop severe bleeding. Period use was temporary; prolonged use risked limb loss (known by experienced healers).  
*Materials:* Leather strap or cord; buckle or toggle.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 round to apply. The referee may rule bleeding stops; after 1 hour or more, the referee may impose risk of limb loss or penalty (at the referee's option). Encumbrance 2 en.

**Amputation knife.**  
*Medieval use:* Heavy blade for field amputation when limb was crushed, infected, or beyond repair. Used by barber-surgeons and military healers; grim but life-saving.  
*Materials:* Wrought iron; heavy, sharp blade; wooden handle.  
*Rules:* Use may require healer or roll; the referee shall determine outcome. The referee may rule amputation prevents death from infection but causes permanent loss (see *Rules Cyclopedia*). Encumbrance 15 en.

**Forceps.**  
*Medieval use:* Metal tongs for removing splinters, arrowheads, or foreign bodies from wounds. Used by barber-surgeons and field healers.  
*Materials:* Wrought iron or bronze; hinged jaws; tips curved or straight.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 turn to attempt removal. The referee may grant +1 to remove object or reduce infection risk (at the referee's option). Encumbrance 5 en.

**Surgical needle.**  
*Medieval use:* Curved metal needle for closing wounds (suturing); used with thread or sinew. Essential for deep or gaping wounds.  
*Materials:* Iron or bronze; curved; eye for thread.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 turn per wound (referee's estimate). The referee may rule suture reduces infection or speeds healing (referee's option). Encumbrance 0 en (negligible).

**Cautery iron.**  
*Medieval use:* Iron rod heated in fire and applied to wound to seal vessels and stop bleeding; also used to burn out infection. Painful but effective in period practice.  
*Materials:* Wrought iron; rod or blade shape; heated in coals.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 round to heat; 1 round to apply. The referee may rule cautery stops bleeding and reduces infection risk; victim may need morale or shock roll (at the referee's option). Encumbrance 10 en.

**Bandage roll.**  
*Medieval use:* Roll of linen (or clean cloth) for multiple dressings; used to bind wounds, hold splints, or pad armour. Standard in camp and field.  
*Materials:* Linen or hemp cloth; rolled; sometimes boiled for cleanliness.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 round per dressing. The referee may rule bandage roll provides 5–10 dressings (the referee shall estimate). Encumbrance 15 en.

**Spirits, 1 flask.**  
*Medieval use:* Strong alcohol (e.g. aqua vitae) for cleaning wound edges and tools before closure; reduced infection in period practice. Also used for pain or shock.  
*Materials:* Distilled spirit; clay or glass flask.  
*Rules:* Capacity 1 flask; 10 en. Use: 1 round to clean wound or tools. The referee may rule spirits +1 to save vs. infection when used before closure (at the referee's option). See Section 23 (Hygiene and Medicine).

**Honey, 1 jar.**  
*Medieval use:* Honey applied to wounds was used as antiseptic and to promote healing in medieval medicine. Kept wound moist and discouraged infection.  
*Materials:* Honey (1 lb jar); earthenware or glass.  
*Rules:* Capacity 1 lb; 15 en. Use: 1 round to apply to wound. The referee may rule honey +1 to save vs. infection or extends freshness of dressing (at the referee's option). See Section 23 and Section 34.

## 38. Navigation

Compass and astrolabe and chart—the way in the fog and the featureless waste. Lose the bearing and the road was lost; the right tool could bring the traveller home. Period-appropriate for 15th-century Europe.

**Lodestone.**  
*Medieval use:* Natural magnetic rock (magnetite)—rubbed along the needle in one direction and the needle pointed north when floating or on pivot. Known in Europe by the 12th–13th century; without it the compass was just a needle.  
*Materials:* Natural lodestone (magnetite); often kept in small bag or box.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 turn to magnetise one needle; effect lasts until needle is struck or heated. Required for making or refreshing a compass. Encumbrance 5 en. See Section 25.

**Compass.**  
*Medieval use:* Magnetised needle on pivot (or floating in water)—north. Sailors and travellers by the 15th century; essential in overcast or featureless terrain. Lose the bearing and the road was lost.  
*Materials:* Iron needle; pivot or water bowl; sometimes wooden or brass case.  
*Rules:* +1 to avoid getting lost in open terrain (at the referee's option). Useless in heavy magnetic disturbance (the referee shall determine). Encumbrance 5 en. See Section 25.

**Astrolabe, simple.**  
*Medieval use:* Instrument for altitude of sun or stars—latitude, time, and navigation. Portable versions by the 15th century; use required skill. The wrong reading could lead the ship astray.  
*Materials:* Brass or wood; engraved plate and alidade (sighting bar).  
*Rules:* Use: 1 turn to take reading (the referee may require roll or skill). The referee may grant +1 to avoid getting lost at sea or in open terrain when used correctly. Encumbrance 25 en.

**Quadrant.**  
*Medieval use:* Simpler than astrolabe—weighted cord and graduated arc for angle (e.g. star altitude). Sailors and surveyors; the right angle could fix the position.  
*Materials:* Wood or brass; graduated arc; plumb line.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 round to measure angle. The referee may rule quadrant grants +1 to navigation or construction when angle matters (at the referee's option). Encumbrance 15 en.

**Hourglass, 1 hour.**  
*Medieval use:* Sand from upper bulb to lower—watches at sea, cooking time, sermon length. Common in ships and households by the 15th century. Tilt it and the measure was false.  
*Materials:* Glass bulbs; wood or metal frame; fine sand.  
*Rules:* Measures 1 hour (or other interval if specified). Must be level; the referee may rule tilt causes inaccurate measure. Encumbrance 15 en. See Section 25.

**Star chart.**  
*Medieval use:* Parchment (or paper) with constellations and sometimes hour lines—navigation at night with astrolabe or naked eye. Wet it and the chart was ruined.  
*Materials:* Parchment or paper; ink; sometimes varnished.  
*Rules:* The referee may grant +1 to avoid getting lost on clear nights when chart is used (at the referee's option). Encumbrance 1 en. Fragile; the referee may rule water damages chart (at the referee's option).

**Ruler, 1'.**  
*Medieval use:* Wood or horn—1 foot for short lengths. Scribes, craftsmen, and merchants; the wrong length could waste material or cheat the buyer.  
*Materials:* Wood (boxwood, maple) or horn; marked in inches or digits.  
*Rules:* Use: 1 round to measure. The referee may grant +1 to construction or copying when exact length matters. Encumbrance 2 en.

**Map case.**  
*Medieval use:* Leather case for maps and charts on the road—often waterproof. Navigators, merchants, and military; the wet map was no map at all.  
*Materials:* Leather (oil-treated); strap closure; sometimes metal trim.  
*Rules:* Holds maps and documents; 15 en. The referee may rule map case waterproof (+1 to save vs. water). See Section 30.

*Medieval use:* Sectional or spare wooden pole for the tent—replace the broken or lost pole. Cautious travellers carried one; without the pole the tent was a rag.  
*Materials:* Wood (ash, spruce); sectional or one piece; 4–6' typical.  
*Rules:* Encumbrance 30 en. Use: 1 turn to replace pole. The referee may rule tent unusable without pole (at the referee's option).

**Guy line, 10'.**  
*Medieval use:* Rope or cord (10')—secure tent or rain fly to pegs. Standard with tent; spare for repair or extra stability. In wind the line held or the tent fell.  
*Materials:* Hemp or flax; 10' length.  
*Rules:* Encumbrance 5 en per line. Use: 1 round to secure to peg. The referee may rule guy line required for tent in wind (at the referee's option).

**Brazier, portable.**  
*Medieval use:* Small metal bowl for coals—heat indoors or in tent. Risk of smoke; ventilation needed in enclosed space. Portable for camp; the closed tent could choke.  
*Materials:* Iron or brass; bowl on feet or stand; 8–12" diameter.  
*Rules:* Encumbrance 40 en (cold); the referee may rule less when empty (e.g. 30 en). Use: 1 turn to light and place. The referee may rule ventilation required in tent (at the referee's option).

**Foot warmer.**  
*Medieval use:* Metal box (often with handle) filled with coals—under the feet in cold. Carriage, tent, or bed; travellers and nobles. The coals died in an hour or two.  
*Materials:* Metal (tin, brass); lidded box; sometimes cloth cover.  
*Rules:* Encumbrance 20 en. The referee may rule foot warmer grants +1 to save vs. cold when used (at the referee's option). Coals last 1–2 hours (the referee shall estimate).
